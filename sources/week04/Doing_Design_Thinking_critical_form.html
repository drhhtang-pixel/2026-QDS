2. Scope & Background 研究範疇與背景

Scope:

Design thinking, Innovation management, Design management, New product development (NPD)

Background:
  • Over the past decade, design thinking has moved from innovation buzzword to widely diffused practice (Brown, 2008; Liedtka, 2015; Martin, 2009), and it has been implemented in firms such as SAP, P&G, Intuit, Bank of America, Samsung and Kaiser Permanente.
  • A generally accepted definition is still lacking, and even the term itself is controversial among its practitioners and advocates (Liedtka, 2015).
  • Design scholars have long written on "designerly ways of thinking" (Buchanan, 1992; Dorst, 2006, 2011; Lawson & Dorst, 2009), whereas management has only recently framed design thinking as an approach to innovation and creative problem solving.
  • An "umbrella construct" that loosely covers diverse phenomena (Hirsch & Levin, 1999) is useful at first, but without clarity it risks construct collapse, as happened to organizational effectiveness and work climate.

3. Research Problem, Aim & Objectives 研究問題、目的與目標

Problem:

What is design thinking — what are its essential attributes, tools and methods — given that promoters and critics disagree about what it is, where it applies, and what it achieves？

Aim / Purpose:

To shed light on current knowledge and conceptualizations of design thinking, so that it does not collapse as a construct and scholars and managers can benefit from its apparent advantages.

Objectives:
  • Identify the principal attributes of design thinking and the tools and methods that support them.
  • Reveal the (sometimes contradictory) perspectives of scholars writing on design thinking, and recommend topics for further study.
  • Surface the enduring questions that may hinder practical implementation, and suggest theoretical frames to address them.

4. Methodology & Steps 研究方法與步驟

Methodology:

Multiphase study: systematic literature review (Tranfield et al., 2003) + card sorting + cluster analysis

Steps:
  1. Phase 1 – Systematic review: searched four databases (ABI ProQuest, Business Source Premier, Science Direct, Emerald), screened articles, and added influential books and applied models.
  2. Two researchers coded the 104 articles, first independently and then jointly, and grouped the codes into attributes.
  3. Phase 2 – Card sorting: seven senior designers described, critiqued and sorted cards of the attributes and tools (member check).
  4. Phase 3 – Cluster analysis: grouped the articles by six attributes (hierarchical clustering, then K-means) and validated the clusters with ANOVA.
  5. Interpreted each cluster as a perspective in the literature and derived research questions, theoretical implications and managerial implications.

(C2.2)

Coding of constructs

  1. Initial review: 91 codes (54 concepts + 37 tools and methods).
  2. Second-order coding with two identical sets of 91 cards: one researcher found 18 attributes, the other 16; 9 were shared.
  3. Inter-rater reliability 58.5%, described as high for an initial coding phase (Miles & Huberman, 1994).
  4. Discussion of discrepancies → 10 attributes + one category "design tools and methods" (37 tools).
Others: Rosenthal & Rosnow (1991)
(C3)

Phase 2: Card sorting exercise

  1. Convenience sample of 7 design directors / senior designers, 15–25 years of experience (2 medical devices, 2 FMCG, 2 industrial products, 1 automotive).
  2. Sorted 10 attribute cards + 37 tool cards; could relabel, merge or add (blank cards); about 45 minutes each.
  3. No attribute discarded, none added; the last two designers added no new insight.
  4. "Problem solving" and "creativity and innovation" were seen as outcomes; user-centeredness and collaboration permeate the whole process.
  5. The 37 tools and methods were merged into 8.
Others: Harloff (2005); Goulding (2002)

These are not new constructs; the design thinking literature gives each a contextually unique meaning and combines them to explain outcomes that no single attribute could. (Plus "design tools and methods": 37 types.)

① Creativity and innovation

Creativity = producing novel and useful ideas; innovation = implementing them (Amabile, 1988). Mentioned in every article — both an attribute and an outcome.

② User-centeredness and involvement

Empathy is the prime means of being user-centered.

Participatory design vs. design thinking: participatory / co-creative design gives end users direct roles in early design phases (Sanders & Stappers, 2008); in design thinking, user needs are considered in many ways without necessarily involving users directly (Beverland et al., 2015).

③ Problem solving

Especially wicked problems (Buchanan, 1992; Rittel in Churchman, 1967): ill-formulated, many clients with conflicting values; they can be addressed but not solved "rightly". An alternative to linear problem solving.

④ Iteration and experimentation

Trial-and-error learning; cycles of problem definition and solution. Prototypes are for learning and finding new directions, not for validation (Brown, 2008).

⑤ Interdisciplinary collaboration

Cross-functional teams make sure the technical, business and human dimensions of a problem are all represented (Glen et al., 2014).

⑥ Ability to visualize

Moving from abstract thinking to visualized ideas and thinking on top of them (Boni et al., 2009). Not necessarily physical — storytelling also visualizes.

⑦ Gestalt view

A holistic, integrative view: the whole is more than the sum of parts; the problem is seen in its system and context, which allows reframing (Drews, 2009).

⑧ Abductive reasoning

The imagination of what might be rather than analysis of what is (Martin, 2010); a best guess that creates new insight (Kolko, 2010); allows reframing (Dorst, 2011).

⑨ Tolerance of ambiguity and failure

Ambiguity is inherent in wicked problems; early, inexpensive failures are valuable for learning (Luchs et al., 2016).

⑩ Blending analysis and intuition

Not rejecting analysis but dynamically balancing it with intuition (Martin, 2009); also exploration/exploitation, reliability/validity.

(C7)

Phase 3: Cluster analysis — selecting variables

  1. Challenge: 10 attributes + divergent views make it hard to say precisely what design thinking is.
  2. "Creativity and innovation" appears in all articles → cannot discriminate.
  3. User-centeredness, problem solving, iteration appear in over 60% → also excluded.
  4. Only six attributes retained (abductive reasoning, ability to visualize, blending, gestalt, interdisciplinary collaboration, tolerance of ambiguity and failure) — 4 of the 10 excluded.
  5. Post hoc: clustering with all 10 put most articles into one large cluster (footnote 4).
(C7.1)

Identifying & validating clusters

  1. Two-stage procedure: hierarchical clustering (dendrogram) to choose the number of clusters, then K-means (Hartigan & Wong, 1979).
  2. Repeated with different algorithms; combined with the researchers' judgment.
  3. A five-cluster model gave the best fit → five perspectives in the literature.
  4. Cluster analysis is exploratory, so some overlap among clusters is expected.
Others: Ketchen & Shook (1996); Hair et al. (1992)
(C7.2)

Validation with ANOVA

  • Compared the five clusters on the three excluded common attributes.
  • User-centeredness F = .10; problem solving F = .90; iteration F = 1.50; all p > .05. Post hoc S-N-K: no significant differences.
  • The five perspectives share the common attributes but differ on the others.

C1 · Interdisciplinary collaboration (38)

"Design thinking is definitely a team sport" (Kelley). Advocates adoption irrespective of industry or problem; all employees should become "design thinkers" (Brown, 2009). Yet claims of benefit remain speculative; some warn of "overblown claims" (McCullagh, 2013).

Brown's (2008) definition actually embeds three skillsets: designer, engineer, business manager.

C2 · Reclaiming design thinking as designers' domain (18)

Mostly non-management authors, critical of management discourse (Kimbell, 2011: the concept presumes all design disciplines share practices). All agree the ability to visualize is central.

Key tension: a way of approaching problems, or a professional skillset? Can non-designers "do" design thinking?

C3 · Resilience in problem-solving (20)

Emphasis on organizational culture: "we'll have to be willing to tolerate more failure" (Nooyi, PepsiCo). An alternative to narrowly analytical approaches, yet managers still tend to seek closed-form solutions.

Design culture (Deserti & Rizzo, 2014) vs. diversity of ambiguity tolerance in teams (Beckman & Barry, 2007).

C4 · Seeing and reflecting upon the whole (14)

Focus on individual designers and the "thinking" part; how to educate designers. Draws on Schön's (1983) The Reflective Practitioner: action and reflection; gestalt view enables reframing.

C5 · Learning to think like a designer (14)

Roger Martin's abductive reasoning and blended logics; non-designers can and should learn it, so teach it in business schools (Dunne & Martin, 2006). Counterpoint: talent and craft cannot be picked up in "a workshop or two" (McCullagh, 2010).

Research questions (examples)

  • C1: What constitutes a "design thinker"? What is its effect on performance?
  • C2: Approach to problems, or skillset requiring training?
  • C3: Does it require a failure-tolerant culture, or create one?
  • C4: How can design thinking skills be assessed?
  • C5: What are the barriers to adoption? Results of teaching it in MBAs?
(C9)

Contributions

  1. 10 attributes + 8 tools and methods rigorously derived from multidisciplinary literature → clarity for a construct "plagued by polysemy".
  2. Identification of five scholarly perspectives → detailed recommendations for further study.
  3. Enduring questions that impede practical implementation, with suggested theoretical frames.
(C9.1)

Theoretical implications

  • Design thinking has the reverse problem of most academic concepts: it lacks rigor, not relevance.
  • Microfoundations: design thinking as Schön's reflective "conversation" with a situation.
  • Communities of practice (Wenger, 1998): designers bristle at "anyone" doing design thinking; managers, with a logic of replication, see it as separable activities.
  • Critiques: Nussbaum (2011) — a "failed experiment" turned into a linear, gated method; Verganti (2017) — "design moved closer to management."
(C9.2)

Managerial implications

  • When? Only the "fuzzy front end" of NPD (Luchs, 2016; Lockwood, 2009b), or the whole process (Deserti & Rizzo, 2014)? Radical or incremental innovation?
  • How? Senior-management commitment and a design-thinking coordinator, or organic cross-team structures?
  • vs. Agile & Lean Startup: they share iteration and user focus, but design thinking mixes activities, skills, orientations and logics (e.g., abductive reframing before searching for solutions).
Others: Ries (2011); Cooper & Sommer (2016)

6. Significance 貢獻與說服力

  • Reliability — logic holds: the problem (a fuzzy construct at risk of collapse) is answered by a method that first derives the attributes (review), then checks them with practitioners (card sorting), then maps disagreement (cluster analysis). Each phase feeds the next, and the results map onto the three objectives.
  • Limits to keep in mind: the card sorting used a convenience sample of 7 product designers (no service/interaction designers, no managers); inter-rater agreement was 58.5% before discussion; clusters rest on the presence/absence of only six attributes; the sample is limited to management discourse and ranked journals.
  • Importance: published in JPIM, it offers a shared vocabulary (10 attributes, 8 tools) that later empirical studies can operationalize, and a concrete research agenda — its central claim is that design thinking research now needs empirical evidence.
  • For this course: a model of a systematic literature review — databases (ABI/INFORM, Business Source Premier; see week 3, tab 7), a search string, inclusion/exclusion rules, a data-gathering figure, and coding with inter-rater reliability.

7. Questions? 討論問題

核心問題

If design thinking is a way of approaching problems that anyone can learn (Clusters 1 & 5), but designers argue it cannot be separated from design doing and visualization (Clusters 2 & 4), which view does your own research take — and how would that choice change whom you study and which method you use?
